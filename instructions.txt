1. The challenge at a glance
You will build a poker bot: a small Python web server that receives a description of the game, decides what to do, and answers with an action. A referee program (the judge) deals the cards, asks each bot for its move, enforces the rules, and keeps score.
The game: heads-up (two players) Short Deck 5-Card Pot-Limit Omaha. It's like Texas Hold'em, but with a 36-card deck, five private cards each, and a rule that you must use exactly two of them.
Your job: fill in three Python functions in one file, agent.py. Everything else, including the web server, is already written for you.
The tournament: every bot plays every other bot once. Each match is 2,000 hands, played so that both bots get exactly the same cards (Section 6).
Winning: a match is won by a clear, statistically significant margin. Close matches are a draw (a "chop"). Most points wins the tournament.
Any approach is allowed: hand-written rules, simulations, statistics, or machine learning. You choose the libraries.
The most important rule
If your bot crashes or breaks the rules too often during a match, your team loses that match. A simple bot that always works beats a clever bot that sometimes crashes. Test thoroughly (Section 8).

3. The game: Short Deck 5-Card Pot-Limit Omaha
This section teaches the game from scratch. If you already play poker, skim it, but read 3.3 (hand rankings are different from normal poker), 3.4 (the Omaha rule) and 3.7 (bet sizes).
3.1 Words you'll see

Blinds
Forced bets before any cards are seen, so there is always something to win. The small blind is 1 chip and the big blind is 2 chips.

A player has put all their chips in. They can't bet further, and the remaining cards are dealt out.

3.2 The deck
Short Deck uses only 36 cards: the ranks 6, 7, 8, 9, T (ten), J, Q, K, A in four suits (clubs c, diamonds d, hearts h, spades s). The 2s, 3s, 4s and 5s are removed. Cards are written as rank then suit, so Ah is the ace of hearts and 6c is the six of clubs.
Inside your code, each card is an integer from 0 to 35:
rank = card >> 2     # 0 = six, 1 = seven, ..., 7 = king, 8 = ace
suit = card & 3      # 0 = clubs, 1 = diamonds, 2 = hearts, 3 = spades
You don't need to remember this: use card_rank(c), card_suit(c) and cards_to_str(cards) from evaluator.py. For example, cards_to_str([35, 0]) gives "As 6c".

Why the changes? With fewer low cards, flushes are rarer than full houses and straights are more common than three of a kind, so the rankings swap to keep rarer hands stronger.
The ace is high and low. The ace normally ranks above the king, but it can also act as the low card in the straight A-6-7-8-9 (the missing 2-5 are skipped). That is the lowest straight: it loses to 6-7-8-9-T. The same goes for straight flushes.
Ties inside the same category are broken by the usual card-by-card comparison (higher pairs first, then the highest remaining cards, called "kickers"). Suits never break ties. Exactly equal hands split the pot.
3.4 The Omaha rule: exactly 2 + 3
At showdown, each player makes their best five-card hand using exactly two of their five hole cards and exactly three of the five board cards. Not one, not three, not four: always two from your hand and three from the board. That gives 10 × 10 = 100 possible hands, and the best one counts.
Example: the rule people get wrong
Board: Ah Kh Qh Jh 6c. You hold Th 7s 7d 8c 9c.
Do you have a royal flush (Ah Kh Qh Jh Th)? No. That would use four board cards and only one hole card. A flush is impossible too: you'd need two hearts in your hand, and you only have one.
Your best hand is Th 9c from your hand plus Kh Qh Jh from the board: 9-T-J-Q-K, a king-high straight.
Lesson: lots of one suit on the board doesn't give you a flush unless two of your hole cards are that suit.
omaha_score(hole_cards, board) applies this rule for you and returns a number: higher is better, equal is a tie. It needs the full five-card board.
3.5 How one hand is played
Setup. Both players start with 80 chips (stacks reset every hand, so a bad hand never carries over). The button posts the small blind (1), the other player the big blind (2). Each player gets 5 hole cards.
Preflop. The button acts first. Betting continues until both players have acted and put in the same amount, or someone folds.
Flop. Three board cards are revealed. Another betting round. From now on the non-button player acts first.
Turn. A fourth board card. Another betting round.
River. The fifth board card. A final betting round.
Showdown. If nobody folded, the higher omaha_score wins the whole pot. Equal hands split it.
A hand ends early if someone folds (the other player wins the pot, and no cards are shown). If a player goes all-in and the bets are matched, there is no more betting: the rest of the board is dealt and the hand goes to showdown.
After a preflop call of the big blind, the big blind still gets to act (check or raise) before the flop. This is called the big blind's option.

RAISE_POT

Both raises are legal only if all of these are true:
Fewer than 4 raises have happened on this street (blinds don't count). After 4, players can only call or fold.

Always pick from obs.legal_actions. It lists exactly what you may do right now.
3.7 How big is a raise?
You never choose an amount. You choose half-pot or pot, and the game works out the amount. Using the values from your observation:
pot_raise_to  = opp_bet + pot + to_call             # the pot-limit maximum
half_raise_to = opp_bet + (pot + to_call) // 2
min_raise_to  = opp_bet + max(last raise size, 2)    # a raise must be at least this big
 
raise_to = min( max(target, min_raise_to), pot_raise_to, my_bet + my_stack )
"Raise to" means your total bet on this street afterwards. In words: your raise is at least the minimum raise, at most a pot-sized raise, and never more than all your chips (if you're short, the raise becomes an all-in). You don't need to compute any of this yourself: obs.raise_to[Action.RAISE_HALF] and obs.raise_to[Action.RAISE_POT] already hold the exact totals.
Worked example
The button (player A) has posted 1 and player B has posted 2, so the pot is 3.

4. Writing your bot
Your whole bot lives in agent.py. The bottom half of the file (marked SERVER PLUMBING) is the web server that talks to the judge. Don't change it: if it breaks, the judge can't talk to your bot. You fill in three functions between # ===== YOUR CODE HERE ===== and # ===== END OF YOUR CODE =====.
4.1 The three functions
Function
When it's called
What you do
new_half(config, memory)
Once at the start of each half-match (1,000 hands).
Set up anything you want to track, e.g. memory["opp_raises"] = 0.
choose_action(obs, memory)
Every time it's your turn.
Look at obs and return one of `obs.legal_actions`.
on_hand_end(result, memory)
Once after every finished hand.
Optional: learn from what happened (e.g. count how often the opponent raises).


Here is a minimal example that shows how the pieces fit together. It always returns a legal action, but it is not a good strategy: in our tests it loses to the random baseline bot. Beating it is your first goal.
def choose_action(obs: Observation, memory: dict) -> Action:
    # Before the river we can't score our hand yet: only call cheap bets.
    if obs.street != Street.RIVER:
        if obs.to_call <= obs.big_blind:          # cheap: stay in the hand
            return Action.CHECK_CALL
        if Action.FOLD in obs.legal_actions:      # too expensive: give up
            return Action.FOLD
        return Action.CHECK_CALL
 
    # On the river all 5 board cards are out, so we can score our hand.
    score = omaha_score(obs.hole_cards, obs.board)
    if score >= (FLUSH << 20) and Action.RAISE_POT in obs.legal_actions:
        return Action.RAISE_POT                  # very strong: bet big
    return Action.CHECK_CALL
(This needs from evaluator import FLUSH and from game import Street at the top. Hand scores keep the hand category in their top bits, so score >> 20 gives the category from 0 = high card to 8 = straight flush. category_name(score) turns it into text like "flush".)

To save time, finished hands are delivered together with your next decision, so on_hand_end always runs before choose_action sees the next hand. The last few hands of a half are delivered when the half ends.
4.4 Remembering things: use memory
Store everything you want to remember in the memory dictionary. It is wiped clean at the start of each half. In the tournament, each half also runs in a brand-new copy of your server, so nothing at all (not memory, not global variables, not files) carries over from one half to the other. Use memory and your bot behaves the same locally and in the tournament.
Reading files that are part of your repository (for example a trained model loaded when the file starts) is fine. See Section 7 for writing files.
4.5 Rules for your code
Always return a value from obs.legal_actions. Anything else counts as an error (Section 5).
Keep choose_action fast. You share a small time budget across a whole half (Section 5).
Your server answers one request at a time, so your functions never run twice at once.
Catch your own mistakes: wrapping your strategy in try/except and falling back to CHECK_CALL is a cheap way to avoid errors.
Don't try to interfere with the judge, the other bot, or the machine. Bots that do will be disqualified.
5. Time limits, errors and forfeits
5.1 The time bank
Your bot has a time bank of 8 seconds of thinking time per half (1,000 hands). Every request the judge sends you is timed, and the time is taken from your bank. The first millisecond of each request is free, to cover network delay.
A single request may take at most 1 second. Taking longer counts as an error.
When your bank runs out, your bot is no longer asked: for the rest of the half it automatically checks when that's free and folds otherwise. That's not an error, but it will lose you a lot of chips.
Do the maths
A half has 1,000 hands and you typically decide 2 to 4 times per hand, so 8 seconds means roughly 2 to 4 milliseconds per decision on average. A full board evaluation with omaha_score takes a few microseconds, so you can afford some simulation, but not thousands of full simulations on every decision. Measure your bot's time with the judge's report (Section 8).
5.2 What counts as an error
Each of these counts as one error, and the judge plays the default action for you instead (check if free, otherwise fold):
Your code raises an exception (in choose_action, on_hand_end or new_half).
You return something that isn't an action, or an action that isn't legal right now.
A request takes longer than 1 second.
Your server can't be reached or sends back a malformed reply.
5.3 Forfeits mean a loss
After 5 errors in one half, your bot forfeits that half: it is never asked again, and the judge plays check-or-fold for it until the half ends. A bot also forfeits if it fails to start or doesn't answer at the beginning of a half.
In the tournament, a forfeit loses the match
If your bot forfeits either half of a match, your team gets a loss (0 points) for that match, however many chips you were winning by. Your opponent gets the win. If both bots forfeit, both teams get a loss.
Running out of time bank is not a forfeit (but it's very costly, see 5.1).
The judge's report shows your error count, and for crashes it prints your bot's full Python traceback, so you can find and fix the bug.
6. How matches and the tournament are scored
6.1 Duplicate format: same cards for both bots
Poker has a lot of luck: a weak bot can win a match just by being dealt better cards. To cancel most of that luck, every match is played in duplicate:
Half 1: 1,000 hands. Your bot sits in one seat, your opponent in the other.
Half 2: the same 1,000 deals again, with the seats swapped. You now get exactly the cards your opponent had, and the same boards.
So over the whole match both bots hold exactly the same cards. Whoever plays them better comes out ahead. Because each half runs in a fresh copy of your bot, you can't remember the cards from the first half.
6.2 Who wins a match
For each of the 1,000 deals, the judge adds up your result from both halves, in big blinds. It then takes the average per deal and a 95% confidence interval around it (average ± 1.96 × standard error).
Confidence interval
Verdict
Points
Entirely above 0
You win
You 1, opponent 0
Entirely below 0
You lose
You 0, opponent 1
Includes or touches 0
Chop (draw): too close to call
0.5 each


In plain words: you only win a match if the judge is confident your edge is real and not luck. Beating a weak bot by a lot is a sure win; two evenly matched bots will usually chop. And remember: a forfeit is always a loss (Section 5.3).
6.3 The tournament
Every bot plays every other bot exactly once (a round robin). Standings are ordered by total points. Ties on points are broken by average big blinds won per deal across all your matches.

10. Strategy ideas
You don't need any of these to take part. They're starting points if you're not sure where to begin.
Beat the baselines first. A bot that folds weak hands, calls with decent ones and raises strong ones already beats random play by a wide margin.
Estimate your chances. Before the river, deal the missing board cards (and a random opponent hand) many times and count how often you win with omaha_score. This is called Monte Carlo equity. Watch your time budget!
Compare price and chances. If you must call 10 chips into a pot that will be 40, you need to win more than 25% of the time to profit.
Preflop shortcuts. Precompute simple features of your five hole cards (pairs, suited cards, connected ranks, high cards) instead of simulating every preflop decision.
Position matters. After the flop the button acts last and sees what the opponent did first. That's an advantage.
Model your opponent. Use on_hand_end and action_history to track how often your opponent raises, folds or bluffs (cards revealed at showdown are gold), then adapt.
Machine learning. You can train offline (self-play or simulation) and ship the trained model in your repository. Remember the 1-CPU, 4 GB, no-internet limits, and keep inference fast.
Robustness beats brilliance. One unhandled exception path, triggered 5 times, loses the whole match.
11. Frequently asked questions
Do stacks carry over from hand to hand?
No. Both players start every hand with 80 chips. Your score is the sum of chips won or lost over all hands.
Can my bot see the opponent's cards?
Only after a showdown, in on_hand_end (result.opp_hole). Never during a hand.
Who is the button?
It alternates every hand. Check obs.is_button.
Can I choose any bet size?
No. Only the four actions exist. The game computes raise amounts (Section 3.7).
Why did my match end in a chop even though I won more chips?
Your winning margin wasn't big enough, compared with the randomness of the cards, for the judge to be 95% confident. Win by more, or more consistently.
Can I use several files or folders?
Yes. agent.py can import your own modules and load data files from your repository.
My bot works locally but failed the build check.
Usually a missing package, a file that wasn't committed, writing outside /tmp, using more than 4 GB of memory, or needing the internet. Check each against Section 7.
Can I edit the server plumbing or the judge?
Changing game.py, evaluator.py or judge_engine.py has no effect in the tournament, which uses its own copies. Changing the plumbing in agent.py risks breaking the connection to the judge, so please don't.
Are the numbers in this guide final?
The settings in this guide (stacks, blinds, time bank and so on) may be tuned before the tournament. If they change, the updated game.py in the starter repository is the source of truth, and we'll announce it.
